Key takeaways

  • Astra distinguishes execution quality without task-specific fine-tuning. Zero-shot ranking τa is 0.8166 and direct preference accuracy is 92.4%, the highest point estimates among the three general-purpose configurations tested here.
  • Context examples have mixed effects. Progress VOC rises from 93.6 to 94.2, while ranking τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. In a bottle-opening example, higher progress correlation also accompanies a worse endpoint estimate.
  • Higher RoboFail accuracy comes with lower failure recall. Accuracy is 85.96% with five examples versus 64.91% zero-shot. False alarms fall from 18 to 2, but misses rise from 2 to 6. Backend consistency is unconfirmed, so the difference cannot be attributed solely to examples.
  • Most of the composite explanation-score gain comes from success recognition. ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499; about 89.3% of the gain comes from the correct-success term, rather than the failure-explanation term.

Introduction

Robot systems need to evaluate their own behavior: assess how far a task has progressed, compare the quality of different executions, and identify and explain failures. General-purpose multimodal models can jointly process visual observations and task descriptions, while receiving evaluation criteria and examples through context, offering a way to make these judgments without additional task-specific fine-tuning. We ask: To what extent can the existing visual understanding and reasoning capabilities of general-purpose multimodal models support the evaluation of robot behavior? How do these capabilities vary across evaluation tasks, available observations, and in-context examples?

We conduct an empirical study with GPT-6 Astra [1] as our primary subject on RBM [2] [3] and RoboFail [4] . We examine trajectory quality scoring, direct preference judgments, task progress estimation, and subtask success and failure judgments with explanations, assessing the model's ability to compare executions, track task progression, and recognize failures. We further compare zero-shot performance with conditions that provide in-context examples, and use exploratory observation-masking cases to examine how responses change with the available visual information.

Our results identify execution-quality comparison as a strength of Astra in this study, but this ability does not translate into equally reliable completion judgments or failure diagnosis. Zero-shot ranking reaches Kendall τa = 0.8166, numerically above the published values of 0.655 for the specialized Robometer model [2] and 0.675 for RynnValue-8B [5] ; direct preference accuracy is 92.4%. On RoboFail, zero-shot and five-example accuracy is 64.91% and 85.96%, respectively, yet missed failures increase from 2 to 6; 89.3% of the composite explanation-score gain comes from correct success judgments. The evidence therefore supports Astra for offline execution-quality comparison, but it still misjudges completion and misses real failures, and higher composite explanation scores do not establish reliable diagnosis of failure causes. Whether these assessments improve policy learning or closed-loop execution remains untested.

1. Study design

1.1 Evaluation tasks and benchmarks

We examine three aspects of general-purpose multimodal models’ ability to evaluate robot behavior: comparing execution quality, estimating task progress from the execution observed so far, and judging subtask outcomes and explaining failures. We use RBM [2] [3] for trajectory ranking, pairwise preference, and progress evaluation, and RoboFail [4] for outcome judgment and explanation. We further investigate how in-context examples and visible observations affect these evaluations.

1.2 Model configurations and evaluation conditions

Our primary subject is GPT-6 Astra [1] , supplied with task descriptions and observations through prompts, without additional evaluation-task fine-tuning. The RBM evaluation also includes zero-shot comparisons with GPT-5.6-sol [6] and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [7] .

We compare zero-shot and in-context conditions for progress estimation, independent ranking, and RoboFail outcome judgment. We also vary the visible observations in two RoboFail cases to examine sensitivity to input information. Tasks are evaluated separately, and published results are presented as contextual references, distinct from our measurements.

Independent ranking: Kendall τa, higher is better. Within a source–task group, the reference quality order is success > suboptimal > failure. For pairs included by the scoring protocol, a correct order contributes +1, an inversion −1, and a predicted tie 0. Reference-quality ties must not be given an invented order; their inclusion follows the scorer. A group score can be written as (C − D) / N, where C and D count concordant and discordant pairs and N is the protocol’s pair count. Ties remain in the denominator, so assigning every trajectory the same score cannot yield a high τa. Aggregation must preserve the scorer’s inclusion rules and weights. We retain reported aggregates rather than interpret weighted decompositions as raw independent-pair counts.

Direct preference: equal-source accuracy, higher is better. Compute the fraction of correct choices within each source, then average the six source accuracies equally. Let cₛ be correct choices and nₛ the evaluated pairs in source s; the six sources contain 1,192 fixed pairs in total. Pooling all correct choices and dividing by 1,192 is not this aggregation. Both trajectories are visible together, unlike independently scored ranking. τa is not accuracy: 0.8166 does not mean 81.66% of preference choices are correct.

Apref = (100 / 6) Σs=1 6 (cₛ / nₛ)

Progress: VOC and time-reference MSE. The RBM definition of VOC uses Pearson correlation. For one trajectory, let pᵢ be the prediction at evaluation position i and tᵢ the time reference from normalized frame position. Compute their correlation; the article reports it on a ×100 scale, higher being better. MSE measures numerical distance from the same time reference, lower being better.

r = Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)(tᵢ − t̄) / √[Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)² · Σᵢ(tᵢ − t̄)²]
VOC = 100 × r
MSE = (1 / m) Σᵢ(pᵢ − tᵢ)²

Here m is the number of evaluation positions. Cross-source aggregation of per-trajectory scores still requires the original scorer’s weights; total sample counts do not establish equal trajectory weighting. The time reference is not a human annotation of semantic completion. High correlation can coexist with consistently low scores or a wrong endpoint judgment. Pearson correlation is undefined for a constant prediction and needs the scorer’s convention, not an invented zero. The five stored predictions and time references in the bottle example reproduce its reported VOC and MSE.

Outcome classification: accuracy, failure recall, false-alarm rate, and miss rate. Treat failure as positive: TP is a correctly detected failure, FN a missed failure, TN a correctly recognized success, and FP a success predicted as failure. Accuracy is (TP + TN) / 57; failure recall is TP / 34; false-alarm rate is FP / 23; and miss rate is FN / 34. All four are reported as percentages. They score the answers, not the robot’s execution success rate.

Failure explanations: AHA composite ROUGE-L, higher is better. A wrong label receives 0, a correct success receives 1, and a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 between the original English explanation and reference. For the latter, let L be the longest common subsequence length of the token sequences: P = L / generated-token count, R = L / reference-token count, and F1 = 2PR / (P + R), with zero for no overlap. Sum scores over 57 questions and divide by 57. The composite mixes success recognition and failure-text overlap. Section 2.5 separates them rather than treating the composite as explanation accuracy.

2. Results

2.1 Trajectory quality and preference

We use RBM robot trajectories to compare how general-purpose multimodal models evaluate execution quality. We test GPT-6 Astra [1] , GPT-5.6-sol [6] , and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [7] on independent trajectory scoring and direct preference judgments with two trajectories shown together. Aggregate results compare the three models; detailed cases use Astra as a representative to examine how scores and explanations relate to the observed executions.

We present zero-shot results here and compare context examples in Section 2.3. Case videos replay model-input frames, not original-speed video.

2.1.1 Trajectory quality ranking

We follow the RBM-EVAL-OOD trajectory quality ranking approach [2] [3] : score trajectories independently and use Kendall τa to measure agreement with the reference order of success > suboptimal > failure within the same task. In the zero-shot setting, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash reach 0.8166, 0.7500, and 0.5300, respectively. All three general-purpose multimodal models distinguish execution quality, with substantial numerical differences in agreement. Astra scores highest in this evaluation.

Astra and GPT-5.6-sol also score numerically above the published results of specialized models RynnValue-8B (0.675) [5] and Robometer (0.655) [2] . These results demonstrate strong offline ranking performance from multimodal models without task-specific fine-tuning.

The following positive examples and error analyses examine how the models judge execution quality.

2.1.2 Direct preference

Direct preference presents two trajectories together and asks the model to select the better execution. Across 1,192 fixed pairs, source-equal accuracy is 92.4%, 90.7%, and 82.2% for Astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, respectively. Their point estimates follow the same order as independent scoring, with a 1.7 percentage-point gap between Astra and GPT-5.6-sol. As a literature reference, Robometer reports 82.5% accuracy [2] .

We first examine two choices that agree with the reference, then cases of disagreement. Astra and DeepSeek receive the same tasks, images, and A/B order; the cases below show their actual choices in pairwise comparisons.

The next three choices disagree with the reference. To interpret them, we first need to ask: by what criteria should the model judge one execution to be better?

Astra–reference preference disagreements: a task goal is not a complete evaluation criterion

“Separate two cups” and “open the bin” specify goals without fully defining completion or how to compare executions. Whether an object must be released, whether it must remain upright, and how opening width should be weighed against final stability all involve evaluative priorities. Even agreement about the visual facts can yield different preferences under different criteria. Cup-separation and green-bin disagreements therefore do not directly establish deficient ranking ability in Astra; they also concern whether its evaluation criteria match the reference annotations. This is an issue of criteria underspecified by the task description, not verified disagreement among human annotators. State-recognition questions, such as whether a zipper is open, must be distinguished from these evaluative choices.

2.1.3 Comparison with prior work

Specialized and general-purpose models acquire behavioral evaluation capabilities through different routes. Robometer [2] combines frame-level progress supervision with trajectory-preference supervision; RynnValue [5] learns value representations through temporal distance. We instead query general-purpose multimodal models directly with task descriptions and visual observations, without additional robot-reward training for this evaluation. Astra reaches ranking τa of 0.8166 and preference accuracy of 92.4%, numerically above Robometer’s reported 0.655 / 82.5%; its ranking also exceeds RynnValue-8B’s reported 0.675. These results show that existing general-purpose capabilities support strong offline execution-quality comparison, a capability not confined to specialized robot reward models.

Selecting the better of two executions and assigning comparable scores to separate trajectories impose different demands. Pairwise preference lets a model inspect both executions together and identify contrasts. Independent ranking requires a compatible scoring scale within a task even when trajectories are evaluated separately. Astra and GPT-5.6-sol differ by 1.7 percentage points in preference accuracy and 0.0666 in ranking τa. These gaps cannot be compared directly across metrics; the tasks probe direct comparison and independent scoring, respectively. The preceding inversions and ties also show that identifying an execution problem in an explanation does not ensure that scores preserve the quality order.

Agreement with reference preferences depends on both discrimination and evaluation criteria. When a task leaves priorities such as release and stability unspecified, disagreement alone does not establish inability to rank. Strong trajectory ranking also does not imply calibrated progress values or a reward ready for policy optimization. Our evidence supports offline comparison by general-purpose models; it does not yet establish matched-sample superiority over published models or training gains. The next section asks whether this performance carries over from comparing executions to estimating how far one execution has progressed.

Protocols and statistical notes for literature comparisons

These literature values were not rerun on our samples. Our independent-ranking run includes 567 trajectories across 41 source–task groups; sample-by-sample alignment with the literature evaluations has not been established. External preference evaluation uses 500 different-quality pairs per OOD source; our study uses 1,192 fixed pairs with incompletely documented composition. Sample, input, and aggregation differences prevent a matched-protocol superiority claim.

Repeat-call variance and confidence intervals are unavailable, so these differences are descriptive rather than significance claims.

2.2 Task progress estimation

Beyond comparing whole executions, we examine whether general-purpose multimodal models can estimate task progress from the instruction and observations available so far. Across 105 successful trajectories, zero-shot VOC is 93.6, 93.5, and 89.7 for Astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, respectively.

Astra and GPT-5.6-sol have similar temporal correlations, both above DeepSeek; Astra remains numerically below Robometer’s reported 94.8 [2] . Strong independent quality ranking does not imply leading progress-estimation performance. VOC measures correlation with normalized time, so it cannot by itself establish correct recognition of completion, stagnation, or regression. The following cases examine these state changes directly.

2.3 Effects of in-context examples (ICL)

We next ask whether reference examples can improve Astra’s assessments. With examples, progress VOC increases slightly, while trajectory-ranking agreement decreases.

Progress estimation. With one same- or similar-task trajectory annotated with stage scores, VOC rises from 93.6 to 94.2, a small increase in overall time correlation.

Trajectory ranking. With three reference trajectories at different score levels, inversions decrease but ties increase. Kendall τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. Under score-consistent weighting, the inversion rate falls from 4.78% to 3.68%, while the tie rate rises from 8.78% to 13.11%.

Overall progress correlation improves slightly, but completion judgments can still worsen on individual trajectories. The bottle-opening example compares zero-shot and single-trajectory-example predictions for the same execution to show this difference.

2.4 Subtask success and failure

We further test Astra’s ability to recognize and explain failures on RoboFail [4] . The evaluation contains 57 subtask questions from 15 trajectory groups and asks the model to determine subtask success from keyframes and explain failures. Reading the judgment alongside its reason allows researchers to inspect the cited evidence and potential errors.

Zero-shot Astra identifies 32 of 34 failed subtasks, reaching failure recall of 94.12%. Overall accuracy is 64.91%, above the reported values for the specially trained AHA-7B (62.5%) [8] and AHA-13B (Llama-2, 64.3%), and close to AHA-13B (Vicuna) at 66.1%. This shows an ability to assess subtask outcomes without task-specific fine-tuning. Its judgments are conservative, however: 18 of 23 successful subtasks are predicted as failures.

With five external in-context learning (ICL) examples, Astra’s overall accuracy reaches 85.96%, above AHA-13B (Vicuna)’s reported 66.1%. Benchmark composite ROUGE-L also rises from the zero-shot score of 0.185 to 0.499, above that model’s 0.290. Successful subtasks incorrectly predicted as failures decrease from 18 to 2, indicating better discrimination of success and failure. However, missed failures also increase from 2 to 6, so higher overall accuracy still involves a tradeoff between false alarms and misses. About 89.3% of the composite ROUGE-L gain comes from recognizing more successful subtasks correctly; explanation quality still requires examination of individual cases.

The literature values above were not rerun on our 57 questions, and exact question sets are unaligned; they are listed separately as context.

An image-free reference always answers “failure.” From the current label counts, it gets 34 / 57 correct, or 59.65%. Zero-shot is 5.26 percentage points higher, recognizing five successes while missing two failures. This label-count deduction shows why accuracy must be read alongside both error types, rather than failure detection alone.

The false-alarm rate on successful subtasks falls from 78.26% to 8.70%, while the miss rate on failures rises from 5.88% to 17.65%. The five-example condition recognizes success more often, but also misses more failures. The practical choice depends on the costs of unnecessary intervention and missed failures, which are not measured here.

Two limitations apply. The five demonstrations are reported as disjoint from test trajectories, which does not establish environment, embodiment, near-duplicate, or pretraining independence. Also, returned model identifiers differ despite the same requested model name and reasoning configuration. Backend snapshot consistency is unconfirmed, so the observed difference does not isolate a causal effect of ICL.

2.5 Failure explanations and scores

Composite ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499 with five examples, but about 89.3% of the gain comes from correctly recognizing successful subtasks. Most of the gain reflects better success recognition, rather than an equivalent improvement in explanations of failure causes.

AHA’s composite scoring rule [8] combines correct success judgments with textual overlap for failure explanations, so the two contributions need to be examined separately.

Composite scoring rule and component calculations

Composite ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499 in the five-example condition. This metric does not measure failure explanations alone. Under the AHA rules [8] , a correct success answer receives 1; a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 against the reference explanation; and a label disagreement receives 0. Scores are summed and divided by 57. Evaluation uses the original English answers, not the Chinese paraphrases in this article.

R = (correct successes + summed explanation scores for correct failures) / 57

The correct-success term rises from 0.0877 to 0.3684. Its increase, 16 / 57 ≈ 0.2807, accounts for about 89.3% of the reported total gain of 0.3144. The failure-explanation term rises from 0.0969 to 0.1305, accounting for the remaining 10.7%. A score of 0.499 therefore does not mean that 49.9% of failure explanations are correct. Most of the gain comes from recognizing success.

Both bars in the decomposition chart use the same 0–0.55 scale, with each component divided by 57. Components and totals are rounded separately and may differ in the last decimal.

The explanation term also depends on which failures are detected and on lexical overlap with reference text. It does not independently distinguish a correct paraphrase from a similarly worded but incorrect diagnosis. Assessing the stated cause requires additional semantic review and physical evidence.

The earlier knob and slicing cases show why labels and explanations need separate review. Neither lexical overlap nor aggregate score changes replace checking the evidence cited for an individual judgment.

3. Discussion and limitations

These results support further study of Astra for offline robot assessment: it distinguishes execution quality, tracks progress, and detects many failures in the evaluated samples. The effects of examples nevertheless differ across metrics. Progress evaluation should include endpoint judgments; ranking should include ties; classification should report false alarms and misses separately; and explanations need validation beyond lexical overlap.

3.1 Limitations

Scope. No policy is trained or controlled using the critic. We do not measure reinforcement-learning sample efficiency, online failure localization, recovery, or closed-loop success. Offline scores do not establish reliable contact-state reasoning, causal diagnosis, or reward-model deployment safety.

Suitability for reinforcement learning remains untested. We have not trained a policy with Astra rewards or tested whether its preferences produce a more effective learned reward model. Correlation and ranking on fixed offline trajectories do not establish reliability during policy updates, distribution shifts, or active reward seeking. This is a central question for the planned closed-loop experiments.

Statistics and experimental control. The 57 RoboFail questions share 15 trajectory groups, and ranking pairs may share trajectories and tasks. Repeat-call variation and sampling uncertainty are not quantified, so small differences are not established improvements. RoboFail backend differences and example-selection effects also require separate control.

Labels and cases. Qualitative cases were selected post hoc and do not estimate the prevalence of failure types. Label ambiguity, limited visual evidence, and model error can all affect outcomes. Without independent blind review, model rationales do not establish that a reference label is wrong. We retain the released labels.

Reproducibility status

The available materials provide evaluation counts, demonstration constructions, aggregates, and selected cases. Full independent reproduction still requires source and sample manifests, preference split composition, input-frame identities and resolution, complete prompts and demonstration identities, requested and returned model versions, run dates, output budgets and sampling settings, and parsing, refusal, retry, and missing-output rules.

Scorer versions, aggregation weights, tie conventions, and per-sample predictions are also needed for score audits and uncertainty estimates that preserve trajectory or source–task dependence. Completing the release would not itself resolve backend or example-selection confounds; missing materials are also not evidence that the underlying experiments are invalid.

3.2 Next: reinforcement learning with critic feedback

We plan to use Astra’s assessments in reinforcement learning to test whether evaluating existing trajectories translates into better policy learning. We will first verify model snapshots, prompts, inputs, and demonstration selection, checking stability through repeated calls and independent annotation. We will then fix policy initialization, the training algorithm, and the environment interaction budget while comparing feedback sources.

Reward and preference pathways. One path converts validated progress or success judgments into rewards for policy optimization. Another uses trajectory preferences to supervise a reward model for policy learning. We will compare the signals separately and test calibration rather than assume that a high offline score makes an effective reward. Whether failure explanations help recovery or data selection requires separate ablations, not an inference from ROUGE-L.

Controls and evaluation. Where available, controls will include sparse environment rewards, hand-written rules or sensor-based success checks, and reproducible learned critics. We will also compare context conditions and feedback interfaces. Evaluation will report success-versus-interaction learning curves, samples needed to reach a given success level, uncertainty across random seeds, and query count, latency, and cost. Final success must be determined by environment criteria or human review independent of Astra, rather than making it both the reward source and sole evaluator.

Errors in the loop. Reinforcement learning changes the states a policy visits and may exploit critic errors. We will check whether ineffective actions, apparent completion, or unnecessarily long behavior earn high rewards, and track how false alarms and misses affect learning and recovery. These reinforcement-learning experiments have not been completed; this article does not report policy gains or sample-efficiency improvements.
