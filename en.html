Key takeaways

  • Astra distinguishes execution quality without task-specific fine-tuning. Zero-shot ranking τa is 0.8166 and direct preference accuracy is 92.4%, the highest point estimates among the three general-purpose configurations tested here.
  • Context examples have mixed effects. Progress VOC rises from 93.6 to 94.2, while ranking τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. In a bottle-opening example, higher progress correlation also accompanies a worse endpoint estimate.
  • Higher RoboFail accuracy comes with lower failure recall. Accuracy is 85.96% with five examples versus 64.91% zero-shot. False alarms fall from 18 to 2, but misses rise from 2 to 6. Backend consistency is unconfirmed, so the difference cannot be attributed solely to examples.
  • Most of the composite explanation-score gain comes from success recognition. ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499; about 89.3% of the gain comes from the correct-success term, rather than the failure-explanation term.

Introduction

Robot systems need to evaluate their own behavior: assess how far a task has progressed, compare the quality of different executions, and identify and explain failures. General-purpose multimodal models can jointly process visual observations and task descriptions, while receiving evaluation criteria and examples through context, offering a way to make these judgments without additional task-specific fine-tuning. We ask: To what extent can the existing visual understanding and reasoning capabilities of general-purpose multimodal models support the evaluation of robot behavior? How do these capabilities vary across evaluation tasks, available observations, and in-context examples?

We conduct an empirical study with GPT-6 Astra [1] as our primary subject on RBM [2] [3] and RoboFail [4] . We examine trajectory quality scoring, direct preference judgments, task progress estimation, and subtask success and failure judgments with explanations, assessing the model's ability to compare executions, track task progression, and recognize failures. We further compare zero-shot performance with conditions that provide in-context examples, and use exploratory observation-masking cases to examine how responses change with the available visual information.

Our results identify execution-quality comparison as a strength of Astra in this study, but this ability does not translate into equally reliable completion judgments or failure diagnosis. Zero-shot ranking reaches Kendall τa = 0.8166, numerically above the published values of 0.655 for the specialized Robometer model [2] and 0.675 for RynnValue-8B [5] ; direct preference accuracy is 92.4%. On RoboFail, zero-shot and five-example accuracy is 64.91% and 85.96%, respectively, yet missed failures increase from 2 to 6; 89.3% of the composite explanation-score gain comes from correct success judgments. The evidence therefore supports Astra for offline execution-quality comparison, but it still misjudges completion and misses real failures, and higher composite explanation scores do not establish reliable diagnosis of failure causes. Whether these assessments improve policy learning or closed-loop execution remains untested.

1. Study design

1.1 Evaluation tasks and benchmarks

We examine three aspects of general-purpose multimodal models’ ability to evaluate robot behavior: comparing execution quality, estimating task progress from the execution observed so far, and judging subtask outcomes and explaining failures. We use RBM [2] [3] for trajectory ranking, pairwise preference, and progress evaluation, and RoboFail [4] for outcome judgment and explanation. We further investigate how in-context examples and visible observations affect these evaluations.

1.2 Model configurations and evaluation conditions

Our primary subject is GPT-6 Astra [1] , supplied with task descriptions and observations through prompts, without additional evaluation-task fine-tuning. The RBM evaluation also includes zero-shot comparisons with GPT-5.6-sol [6] and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [7] .

We compare zero-shot and in-context conditions for progress estimation, independent ranking, and RoboFail outcome judgment. We also vary the visible observations in two RoboFail cases to examine sensitivity to input information. Tasks are evaluated separately, and published results are presented as contextual references, distinct from our measurements.

Independent ranking: Kendall τa, higher is better. Within a source–task group, the reference quality order is success > suboptimal > failure. For pairs included by the scoring protocol, a correct order contributes +1, an inversion −1, and a predicted tie 0. Reference-quality ties must not be given an invented order; their inclusion follows the scorer. A group score can be written as (C − D) / N, where C and D count concordant and discordant pairs and N is the protocol’s pair count. Ties remain in the denominator, so assigning every trajectory the same score cannot yield a high τa. Aggregation must preserve the scorer’s inclusion rules and weights. We retain reported aggregates rather than interpret weighted decompositions as raw independent-pair counts.

Direct preference: equal-source accuracy, higher is better. Compute the fraction of correct choices within each source, then average the six source accuracies equally. Let cₛ be correct choices and nₛ the evaluated pairs in source s; the six sources contain 1,192 fixed pairs in total. Pooling all correct choices and dividing by 1,192 is not this aggregation. Both trajectories are visible together, unlike independently scored ranking. τa is not accuracy: 0.8166 does not mean 81.66% of preference choices are correct.

Apref = (100 / 6) Σs=1 6 (cₛ / nₛ)

Progress: VOC and time-reference MSE. The RBM definition of VOC uses Pearson correlation. For one trajectory, let pᵢ be the prediction at evaluation position i and tᵢ the time reference from normalized frame position. Compute their correlation; the article reports it on a ×100 scale, higher being better. MSE measures numerical distance from the same time reference, lower being better.

r = Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)(tᵢ − t̄) / √[Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)² · Σᵢ(tᵢ − t̄)²]
VOC = 100 × r
MSE = (1 / m) Σᵢ(pᵢ − tᵢ)²

Here m is the number of evaluation positions. Cross-source aggregation of per-trajectory scores still requires the original scorer’s weights; total sample counts do not establish equal trajectory weighting. The time reference is not a human annotation of semantic completion. High correlation can coexist with consistently low scores or a wrong endpoint judgment. Pearson correlation is undefined for a constant prediction and needs the scorer’s convention, not an invented zero. The five stored predictions and time references in the bottle example reproduce its reported VOC and MSE.

Outcome classification: accuracy, failure recall, false-alarm rate, and miss rate. Treat failure as positive: TP is a correctly detected failure, FN a missed failure, TN a correctly recognized success, and FP a success predicted as failure. Accuracy is (TP + TN) / 57; failure recall is TP / 34; false-alarm rate is FP / 23; and miss rate is FN / 34. All four are reported as percentages. They score the answers, not the robot’s execution success rate.

Failure explanations: AHA composite ROUGE-L, higher is better. A wrong label receives 0, a correct success receives 1, and a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 between the original English explanation and reference. For the latter, let L be the longest common subsequence length of the token sequences: P = L / generated-token count, R = L / reference-token count, and F1 = 2PR / (P + R), with zero for no overlap. Sum scores over 57 questions and divide by 57. The composite mixes success recognition and failure-text overlap. Section 2.5 separates them rather than treating the composite as explanation accuracy.

2. Results

2.1 Trajectory quality and preference

We use RBM robot trajectories to compare how general-purpose multimodal models evaluate execution quality. We test GPT-6 Astra [1] , GPT-5.6-sol [6] , and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [7] on independent trajectory scoring and direct preference judgments with two trajectories shown together. Aggregate results compare the three models; detailed cases use Astra as a representative to examine how scores and explanations relate to the observed executions.

We present zero-shot results here and compare context examples in Section 2.3. Case videos replay model-input frames, not original-speed video.

2.1.1 Trajectory quality ranking

We follow the RBM-EVAL-OOD trajectory quality ranking approach [2] [3] : score trajectories independently and use Kendall τa to measure agreement with the reference order of success > suboptimal > failure within the same task. In the zero-shot setting, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash reach 0.8166, 0.7500, and 0.5300, respectively. All three general-purpose multimodal models distinguish execution quality, with substantial numerical differences in agreement. Astra scores highest in this evaluation.

Astra and GPT-5.6-sol also score numerically above the published results of specialized models RynnValue-8B (0.675) [5] and Robometer (0.655) [2] .

The following positive examples and error analyses examine how the models judge execution quality.

2.1.2 Direct preference

Direct preference presents two trajectories together and asks the model to select the better execution. Across 1,192 fixed pairs, source-equal accuracy is 92.4%, 90.7%, and 82.2% for Astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, respectively. Their point estimates follow the same order as independent scoring, with a 1.7 percentage-point gap between Astra and GPT-5.6-sol. As a literature reference, Robometer reports 82.5% accuracy [2] .

We first examine two choices that agree with the reference, then cases of disagreement. Astra and DeepSeek receive the same tasks, images, and A/B order; the cases below show their actual choices in pairwise comparisons.

The following cases examine the evidence and evaluation criteria underlying Astra’s disagreements with reference preferences.

Astra–reference preference disagreements: multidimensional evaluation and criterion alignment

Task descriptions usually specify a goal without fully defining execution quality. The cup-separation and green-bin disagreements involve several dimensions: whether the target state is reached, whether the object is released, whether the final state is stable, and how these requirements are prioritized. A single success label or overall preference compresses these dimensions into one judgment, making it difficult to distinguish disagreements about visual recognition, completion conditions, and trade-offs among quality dimensions. Such reference disagreements therefore do not directly establish deficient ranking ability in Astra.

A more informative analysis would specify task-relevant criteria, annotate the observable evidence and degree of satisfaction for each, and then form an overall judgment using predefined requirements or weights. Cup separation could be assessed through “separated,” “released,” and “upright”; bin opening through “required opening reached” and “remains open without support.” This criterion-based assessment follows the idea of rubric rewards; Flora-Rewarder already provides an interface that outputs a score for each rubric step [14] . It makes the basis of evaluation explicit, while the criteria and weights still depend on task requirements. We have not performed such criterion-level reannotation in this study.

Sampling, metrics, and cited versions

Ranking and different-quality preference evaluation reuse Robometer’s official sampling and metric rules with seed=44. Of 571 cached trajectories, four belong to tasks with only successful examples and are excluded by the official rule, leaving 567. Preference sampling uses up to 500 pairs per source, yielding 1,192 pairs. Both metrics use equal weighting across the six sources.

The current official default seed is 42; the original paper’s historical manifests are unavailable, so this is not a sample-by-sample reproduction. Both sample eight frames uniformly. Astra returns prompted scores or A/B choices, whereas Robometer uses trained progress/preference heads, with different image encoding and internal processing. We cite Robometer v1’s τa=0.655; the corresponding v2 value is approximately 0.643.

Summary. General-purpose multimodal models can effectively compare robot execution quality, with Astra’s ranking and preference results competitive with specialized models. Some reference disagreements involve different priorities among goal attainment, execution constraints, and final-state quality. These require explicit criteria rather than being attributed entirely to deficient ranking ability.

2.2 Task progress estimation

Beyond comparing whole executions, we examine whether general-purpose multimodal models can estimate task progress from the instruction and observations available so far. Across 105 successful trajectories, zero-shot VOC is 93.6, 93.5, and 89.7 for Astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, respectively.

Astra and GPT-5.6-sol have similar temporal correlations, both above DeepSeek; Astra is below Robometer’s reported 94.8 [2] . The following cases examine how scores respond to completion, setbacks, and retries.

2.3 Effects of in-context examples (ICL)

We next ask whether reference examples improve Astra’s progress estimation and trajectory ranking.

Progress estimation. With one same- or similar-task trajectory annotated with stage scores, VOC rises from 93.6 to 94.2, a small increase in overall time correlation.

Trajectory ranking. With three reference trajectories at different score levels, inversions decrease but ties increase. Kendall τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. Under score-consistent weighting, the inversion rate falls from 4.78% to 3.68%, while the tie rate rises from 8.78% to 13.11%.

Overall progress correlation improves slightly, but completion judgments can still worsen on individual trajectories. The bottle-opening example compares zero-shot and single-trajectory-example predictions for the same execution to show this difference.

2.5 Failure explanations and scores

Composite ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499 with five examples, but about 89.3% of the gain comes from correctly recognizing successful subtasks.

AHA’s composite scoring rule [8] combines correct success judgments with textual overlap for failure explanations, so the two contributions need to be examined separately.

Composite scoring rule and component calculations

Composite ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499 in the five-example condition. This metric does not measure failure explanations alone. Under the AHA rules [8] , a correct success answer receives 1; a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 against the reference explanation; and a label disagreement receives 0. Scores are summed and divided by 57. Evaluation uses the original English answers, not the Chinese paraphrases in this article.

R = (correct successes + summed explanation scores for correct failures) / 57

The correct-success term rises from 0.0877 to 0.3684. Its increase, 16 / 57 ≈ 0.2807, accounts for about 89.3% of the reported total gain of 0.3144. The failure-explanation term rises from 0.0969 to 0.1305, accounting for the remaining 10.7%. A score of 0.499 therefore does not mean that 49.9% of failure explanations are correct. Most of the gain comes from recognizing success.

Both bars in the decomposition chart use the same 0–0.55 scale, with each component divided by 57. Components and totals are rounded separately and may differ in the last decimal.

Summary. Most of the composite ROUGE-L gain comes from success recognition, not an equivalent improvement in failure explanations. The knob and slicing cases show why labels and reasons need separate checks; credible causal explanations require semantic and physical evidence.

3. Discussion and outlook

3.1 What the study establishes

General-purpose multimodal models’ existing visual understanding and reasoning can support robot execution-quality comparisons, progress estimation, and outcome judgments. In this evaluation, Astra’s ranking and preference results are competitive with specialized models. Cases also show recognition of target objects, setbacks, and completion after retries, without additional fine-tuning for these tasks.

These capabilities respond differently to the evaluation interface and context. Examples can improve some judgments while changing score scales, increasing ties, or disrupting endpoint judgments. Comparison, progress, outcome classification, and explanation should therefore be evaluated separately; a single aggregate score does not establish a generally reliable robot critic.

3.2 How and when Astra fails

Similar outcomes can obscure quality differences. In the blue-bin case, Astra gives full scores to executions labeled successful and suboptimal. It recognizes the shared outcome of opening the lid but does not distinguish the reference quality levels.

A plausible overall trend can coexist with an incorrect endpoint. In the bottle-opening case, the example condition improves temporal correlation but reduces endpoint progress for a successful trajectory from 1.00 to 0.35. A smooth trend cannot substitute for identifying the decisive state.

A correct outcome label does not guarantee a credible explanation. For the knob grasp, the zero-shot response matches the failure label but gives a different cause from the reference; the five-example condition predicts success instead. By contrast, the cup and bin preference disagreements involve unspecified criteria such as release and stability, and should be analyzed separately from state-assessment errors.

3.3 Limitations and future work

This study is limited to fixed offline trajectories and a restricted set of models and tasks. The 57 RoboFail questions share 15 trajectory groups, and trajectory pairs share tasks and samples. Repeat-call and sampling uncertainty remain unquantified, and some example comparisons have backend differences. Future evaluations should fix model snapshots and demonstration-selection rules and test stability on independent tasks.

Cases were selected post hoc and do not estimate error frequencies. Ambiguous samples need explicit rubrics for goals, execution constraints, and final-state quality, with independent review by multiple annotators. Contact and stability states that images alone cannot resolve require additional viewpoints or sensor evidence to distinguish criterion disagreement, limited observation, and model error.

Reinforcement-learning validation. We will compare calibrated progress or outcome rewards with reward models trained from trajectory preferences. With policy initialization, algorithms, and interaction budgets fixed, we will compare against sparse environment rewards and specialized critics, reporting success, sample efficiency, and query costs across seeds. Independent environment criteria or human review will determine success, while checks for reward exploitation will examine apparent completion and other critic errors. These closed-loop experiments have not yet been conducted; this article reports no policy gains.
