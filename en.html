Key takeaways

  • gpt-6-astra distinguishes execution quality without task-specific fine-tuning. Zero-shot ranking τa is 0.8166 and direct preference accuracy is 92.4%, the highest point estimates among the three general-purpose configurations tested here.
  • Context examples have mixed effects. Progress VOC rises from 93.6 to 94.2, while ranking τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. In a bottle-opening example, higher progress correlation also accompanies a worse endpoint estimate.
  • Higher RoboFail accuracy comes with lower failure recall. Accuracy is 85.96% with five-example ICL versus 64.91% zero-shot. False alarms fall from 18 to 2, but misses rise from 2 to 6.
  • Most of the composite explanation-score gain comes from success recognition. ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499; about 89.3% of the gain comes from the correct-success term, rather than the failure-explanation term.

Introduction

Robot systems need to evaluate their own behavior: assess how far a task has progressed, compare the quality of different executions, and identify and explain failures. General-purpose multimodal models can jointly process visual observations and task descriptions, while receiving evaluation criteria and examples through context, offering a way to make these judgments without additional task-specific fine-tuning. We ask: To what extent can the existing visual understanding and reasoning capabilities of general-purpose multimodal models support the evaluation of robot behavior? How do these capabilities vary across evaluation tasks, available observations, and in-context examples?

We conduct an empirical study with gpt-6-astra [1] as our primary subject on RBM [2] [3] and RoboFail [4] . We examine trajectory quality scoring, direct preference judgments, task progress estimation, and subtask success and failure judgments with explanations, assessing the model's ability to compare executions, track task progression, and recognize failures. We further compare zero-shot performance with conditions that provide in-context examples, and use exploratory observation-masking cases to examine how responses change with the available visual information.

Our results identify execution-quality comparison as a strength of gpt-6-astra in this study, but this ability does not translate into equally reliable completion judgments or failure diagnosis. Zero-shot ranking reaches Kendall τa = 0.8166, numerically above the published values of 0.655 for the specialized Robometer model [2] and 0.675 for RynnValue-8B [5] ; direct preference accuracy is 92.4%. On RoboFail, zero-shot and five-example ICL accuracy is 64.91% and 85.96%, respectively, yet missed failures increase from 2 to 6; 89.3% of the composite explanation-score gain comes from correct success judgments. The evidence therefore supports gpt-6-astra for offline execution-quality comparison, but it still misjudges completion and misses real failures, and higher composite explanation scores do not establish reliable diagnosis of failure causes. Whether these assessments improve policy learning or closed-loop execution remains untested.

1. Study design

1.1 Evaluation tasks and benchmarks

We evaluate trajectory ranking, pairwise preference, and progress estimation on RBM [2] [3] , and subtask outcomes and failure explanations on RoboFail [4] .

1.2 Model configurations and evaluation conditions

Our primary subject is gpt-6-astra [1] , supplied with task descriptions and observations through prompts, without additional evaluation-task fine-tuning. The RBM evaluation also includes zero-shot comparisons with GPT-5.6-sol [6] and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [7] .

Progress estimation, independent ranking, and RoboFail also include in-context examples. Two RoboFail cases additionally compare responses across different numbers of observed frames.

2. Results

2.1 Trajectory quality and preference

We compare independent trajectory scores and direct preference judgments from gpt-6-astra [1] , GPT-5.6-sol [6] , and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [7] , with gpt-6-astra cases illustrating the basis of its judgments.

Videos in this section replay the eight model-input frames.

2.1.1 Trajectory quality ranking

We follow the RBM-EVAL-OOD trajectory quality ranking approach [2] [3] : score trajectories independently and use Kendall τa to measure agreement with the reference order of success > suboptimal > failure within the same task. In the zero-shot setting, gpt-6-astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash reach 0.8166, 0.7500, and 0.5300, respectively. All three general-purpose multimodal models distinguish execution quality, with substantial numerical differences in agreement. gpt-6-astra scores highest in this evaluation.

gpt-6-astra and GPT-5.6-sol also score numerically above the published results of specialized models RynnValue-8B (0.675) [5] and Robometer (0.655) [2] .

Trajectory ranking. With three reference trajectories at different score levels, inversions decrease but ties increase. Kendall τa falls from 0.8166 to 0.7953. Under score-consistent weighting, the inversion rate falls from 4.78% to 3.68%, while the tie rate rises from 8.78% to 13.11%.

2.1.2 Direct preference

Direct preference presents two trajectories together and asks the model to select the better execution. Across 1,192 fixed pairs, source-equal accuracy is 92.4%, 90.7%, and 82.2% for gpt-6-astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, respectively. Their point estimates follow the same order as independent scoring, with a 1.7 percentage-point gap between gpt-6-astra and GPT-5.6-sol. As a literature reference, Robometer reports 82.5% accuracy [2] .

gpt-6-astra and DeepSeek receive the same task, images, and A/B order.

2.1.3 gpt-6-astra–reference preference disagreements: multidimensional criteria and alignment

The cup-separation and green-bin disagreements concern release, upright orientation, and stability—requirements whose priority the task descriptions do not specify.

These requirements can form separate rubric items, such as “separated,” “released,” and “upright,” before an overall judgment is made. Flora-Rewarder offers stepwise rubric scoring [14] ; the cases here retain their original labels.

Sampling, metrics, and cited versions

Ranking and different-quality preference evaluation reuse Robometer’s official sampling and metric rules with seed=44. Of 571 cached trajectories, four belong to tasks with only successful examples and are excluded by the official rule, leaving 567. Preference sampling uses up to 500 pairs per source, yielding 1,192 pairs. Both metrics use equal weighting across the six sources.

This study uses seed=44; the current official default is 42. Both sample eight frames uniformly. gpt-6-astra produces prompted scores or A/B choices, while Robometer uses trained progress/preference heads. We cite Robometer v1’s τa=0.655; the corresponding v2 value is approximately 0.643.

2.1.4 Summary

gpt-6-astra’s ranking and preference results are competitive with specialized reward models, but reference disagreements do not all indicate an inability to compare execution quality.

Inversions and ties account for some lost points; the cup and green-bin cases involve different quality requirements. Explicit release, uprightness, and stability criteria would help distinguish state-recognition errors from disagreements over standards.

2.2 Task progress estimation

2.2.1 Zero-shot progress estimation

Beyond comparing whole executions, we examine whether general-purpose multimodal models can estimate task progress from the instruction and observations available so far. Across 105 successful trajectories, zero-shot VOC is 93.6, 93.5, and 89.7 for gpt-6-astra, GPT-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, respectively.

gpt-6-astra and GPT-5.6-sol have similar temporal correlations, both above DeepSeek; gpt-6-astra is below Robometer’s reported 94.8 [2] . The following cases examine how scores respond to completion, setbacks, and retries.

2.2.3 Summary

2.3.3 In-context learning (ICL)

We use the evaluation’s fixed set of five external demonstrations for in-context learning (ICL). Each contains an image, a subtask question, and a reference answer and is supplied before the test subtask. The model then judges the test subtask’s success or failure and provides a textual explanation; zero-shot prompting omits the demonstrations.

The five-example ICL condition produces fewer false alarms but still misses failures.False alarms fall from 18 to 2, while missed failures rise from 2 to 6. The knob and slicing cases also compare the first frame, first two frames, and all observations, with one request per condition.

2.4 Failure explanations and scores

This section evaluates explanation scores from the same zero-shot and five-example ICL responses as Section 2.3. ICL uses the same fixed set of external image–question–answer demonstrations.

2.4.1 Overall performance and baseline comparison

Composite ROUGE-L is 0.499 with five-example ICL versus 0.185 zero-shot. The success-recognition term rises from 0.0877 to 0.3684 (+0.2807), and the failure-explanation term from 0.0969 to 0.1305 (+0.0336).

gpt-6-astra’s five-example ICL composite score is above the AHA-reported values for AHA-13B Vicuna (0.290), AHA-13B Llama-2 (0.280), AHA-7B (0.204), and five-shot GPT-4o (0.236) [8] .

Composite scoring rule and component calculations

Composite ROUGE-L rises from 0.185 to 0.499 in the five-example ICL condition. This metric does not measure failure explanations alone. Under the AHA rules [8] , a correct success answer receives 1; a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 against the reference explanation; and a label disagreement receives 0. Scores are summed and divided by 57. Evaluation uses the original English answers, not the Chinese paraphrases in this article.

R = (correct successes + summed explanation scores for correct failures) / 57

The correct-success term rises from 0.0877 to 0.3684. Its increase, 16 / 57 ≈ 0.2807, accounts for about 89.3% of the reported total gain of 0.3144. The failure-explanation term rises from 0.0969 to 0.1305, accounting for the remaining 10.7%. A score of 0.499 therefore does not mean that 49.9% of failure explanations are correct. Most of the gain comes from recognizing success.

2.4.2 Failure-explanation quality

The failure-explanation term rises from 0.0969 to 0.1305 (+0.0336) with five-example ICL. This term sums explanation ROUGE-L for correctly detected failures and divides by all 57 questions. Meanwhile, correct detections among the 34 failure questions fall from 32 to 28: the higher explanation term does not come from detecting more failures.

3. Discussion and outlook

3.1 Main findings

gpt-6-astra is competitive with specialized models at ranking and preference judgment. gpt-6-astra’s zero-shot ranking and direct-preference results are numerically above the cited specialized-model results, without additional fine-tuning for the evaluation tasks.

Progress scores track advancement, setbacks, and retries. In the blue-cup and banana-transfer cases, gpt-6-astra’s scores rise with successful placement, fall after slipping or ineffective attempts, and recover when a retry completes the task.

Five-example ICL RoboFail outcome accuracy reaches 85.96%. Correct recognition of the 23 successful subtasks rises from 5 to 21.

The five-example ICL composite explanation score reaches 0.499, above the listed specialized-model reports. Both success-recognition and failure-explanation terms improve; in the knife case, the response also moves from a generic failed grasp to the specific wrong-object error.

3.2 How and when gpt-6-astra fails

Quality distinctions can be too coarse. The blue-bin case receives full scores for both successful and suboptimal executions.

Final-state recognition remains unreliable. In the bottle-opening case, correlation across five score positions improves, but the successful endpoint receives only 0.35.

Failure diagnosis can miss the specific error. In the knife case, zero-shot only reports a failed knife grasp; the five-example ICL answer identifies a spoon, matching the reference’s wrong-object diagnosis.

Ranking errors and missed failures remain with examples. More tied trajectory scores accompany a fall in τa from 0.8166 to 0.7953, while missed RoboFail failures rise from 2 to 6.

3.3 Limitations and future work

This study uses fixed offline trajectories and a limited set of models and tasks. The 57 RoboFail questions come from 15 trajectory groups. Repeated calls and independent tasks are needed to assess stability.

Future work will specify criteria for task goals, execution constraints, and final-state quality, with review by multiple annotators. Additional views or sensors can help assess contact and stability that images alone leave ambiguous.

Reinforcement-learning validation. We will compare calibrated progress or outcome rewards with reward models trained from trajectory preferences. With policy initialization, algorithms, and interaction budgets fixed, we will compare against sparse environment rewards and specialized critics, reporting success, sample efficiency, and query costs across seeds. Independent environment criteria or human review will determine success, while checks for reward exploitation will examine apparent completion and other critic errors. These closed-loop experiments have not yet been conducted; this article reports no policy gains.

Independent ranking: Kendall τa. Within each source–task group, the reference order is success > suboptimal > failure. For pairs included by the official scorer, correct orders contribute +1, inversions −1, and ties 0, giving (C − D) / N. Scores are averaged across tasks and then equally across the six sources.

Direct preference: equal-source accuracy. Accuracy is computed within each source and then averaged equally across six sources. Here cₛ is the number of correct choices and nₛ the number of evaluated pairs in source s, with 1,192 pairs in total.

Apref = (100 / 6) Σs=1 6 (cₛ / nₛ)

Progress: VOC and time-reference MSE. The RBM definition of VOC uses Pearson correlation. For one trajectory, let pᵢ be the prediction at evaluation position i and tᵢ the time reference from normalized frame position. Compute their correlation; the article reports it on a ×100 scale, higher being better. MSE measures numerical distance from the same time reference, lower being better.

r = Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)(tᵢ − t̄) / √[Σᵢ(pᵢ − p̄)² · Σᵢ(tᵢ − t̄)²]
VOC = 100 × r
MSE = (1 / m) Σᵢ(pᵢ − tᵢ)²

Here m is the number of evaluation positions; cross-source aggregation follows the official scorer. The reference is normalized frame position, not human-annotated semantic completion.

Outcome classification: accuracy, failure recall, false-alarm rate, and miss rate. Treat failure as positive: TP is a correctly detected failure, FN a missed failure, TN a correctly recognized success, and FP a success predicted as failure. Accuracy is (TP + TN) / 57; failure recall is TP / 34; false-alarm rate is FP / 23; and miss rate is FN / 34. All four are reported as percentages. They score the answers, not the robot’s execution success rate.

Failure explanations: AHA composite ROUGE-L, higher is better. A wrong label receives 0, a correct success receives 1, and a correct failure receives stemmed ROUGE-L F1 between the original English explanation and reference. For the latter, let L be the longest common subsequence length of the token sequences: P = L / generated-token count, R = L / reference-token count, and F1 = 2PR / (P + R), with zero for no overlap. Sum scores over 57 questions and divide by 57. The composite mixes success recognition and failure-text overlap. Section 2.4 separates them rather than treating the composite as explanation accuracy.
